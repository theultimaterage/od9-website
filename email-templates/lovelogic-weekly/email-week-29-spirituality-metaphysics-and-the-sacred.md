# What you treat as sacred

Hey {{FIRST_NAME}},

Take the word "sacred" out of religious framing for a minute.

The functional definition: **something is sacred to you if you would feel a deep wrongness in violating it — even when violating it would be convenient, profitable, or socially rewarded.**

By that definition, name one thing that's sacred to *you*. Not in word. In practice. Something you've actually structured your life around honoring, even when doing so cost you something real.

Some honest answers I've heard:

- *"Telling the truth to my kids, even when a lie would be easier and they'd never know."*
- *"Never publishing work I don't believe in, even when it would pay the rent."*
- *"Showing up for my best friend's hard moments — every time, no exceptions, no matter what."*
- *"Refusing to take consulting money from companies whose products I'd warn my own family against."*
- *"Sitting with my dying parent every day for the last six weeks of his life — not because I had to, because he'd done it for his."*

Notice none of those are explicitly religious. They're all moral architecture. They're sacred *because the person treats them as non-negotiable.*

Now here's the harder question. The dominant culture tends to treat almost nothing as sacred — everything is up for negotiation, every value is contextually-priced. **What would change about civilization if more people had even ONE thing they wouldn't trade?**

## What the Manifesto pushes on

[**Ch.3**](https://offda9.com/curriculum/observer/love-as-infrastructure.php) of the Manifesto and **Ch.46** take a position that may surprise people who came to OD9 expecting pure rationalism: **the sacred is not optional infrastructure for human civilization.** Even fully secular societies have sacred categories — they just often refuse to admit which ones, which means the sacred categories drift unconsciously and end up serving whoever has cultural power that decade.

A Type-I civilization that doesn't deliberately curate its sacred categories ends up with the sacred categories chosen for it by markets, algorithms, or ambient politics. None of those are good at this. All of them produce sacred categories that serve their own optimization, not human flourishing.

OD9's bet, partial and tentative: **the discipline of identifying what we hold sacred — and defending it operationally — is one of the few practices that scales across the kinds of difference a successful Type-I civilization will need to coordinate across.**

## This week's question

Sunday's deep-dive QOTD:

> *Something you treat as sacred (in practice, not just in word) that the dominant culture doesn't take seriously. What is it? What would change about civilization if more people did?*

The "in practice, not just in word" part is the test. Anyone can claim to value something. The thing you structure your life around is what's actually sacred to you.
