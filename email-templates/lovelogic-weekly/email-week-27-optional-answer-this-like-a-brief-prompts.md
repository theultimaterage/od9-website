# 30 minutes. Three rules.

Hey {{FIRST_NAME}},

Imagine this. A coalition is building a new city from scratch. 50,000 people. Real budget. Blank legal slate — they get to write their own rules within reason. They've heard about OD9 and they want to consult.

You get 30 minutes with the founding team. They will listen carefully. They will not, by default, fight you on anything. **What three rules do you fight hardest to install before you leave the room?**

Not "what would I want to talk about." Not "what would be a fun conversation." What rules do you genuinely think you'd regret not pushing through, if a year later you came back and saw what they'd built without your three?

This is harder than it sounds because:
- You can't push more than three. The founders will quietly forget anything past the third.
- Each rule has to be **operational**. "Be fair" doesn't count. "Every contract over $50k must be public-record by default within 30 days" counts.
- Each rule has to be **enforceable** by the institutions you're not designing in this conversation. So it has to either self-enforce or fit cleanly into known institutional shapes.
- Each rule has to **survive bad-actor stress-testing**. You're choosing what to install knowing the city will eventually have grifters, narcissists, and people optimizing against your design.

Some rules I've heard people try:
- *"Every elected position has a hard term limit. No exceptions."*
- *"All housing density decisions are made by a citizen jury, not the city council."*
- *"Public infrastructure expenditures must publish unit costs comparable to other cities, on a public dashboard, monthly."*
- *"School curricula are decided at the school level, not the district level."*
- *"Taxation is local-by-default; the city can vote to upstream specific functions but never the reverse."*

None of those are obviously right. All of them have failure modes. That's the point — picking three forces you to defend tradeoffs you'd usually avoid.

## What the Manifesto pushes on

**Ch.7** (*Power, Legibility, and Governance*) and **Ch.65** of the Manifesto take a position that most political theory dodges: **the *texture* of civic rules — whether enforcement is automatic or discretionary, whether transparency is default or opt-in, whether decisions are reviewable or final — matters more than the rules' specific content.** Two cities with identical rules but different enforcement texture end up living in completely different worlds.

So the work isn't picking the "right" three rules. It's picking three rules whose *texture* is right — that self-enforce, that're hard to capture, that survive bad actors. That's the actual founding-team conversation worth having.
