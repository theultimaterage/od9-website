# Where authority actually lives

Hey {{FIRST_NAME}},

Pick one. Just one.

Pick **one decision** currently being made by a CEO, a politician, or a landlord — and move it to a community vote.

- The CEO who decides who gets laid off.
- The mayor who decides which neighborhoods get the new bus line.
- The landlord who decides whether to renew your lease.
- The platform owner who decides which posts the algorithm shows.
- The insurance executive who decides which treatments are "covered."

Pick one. Now imagine it's actually a vote. The community most affected by the decision votes on it. Direct, binding.

What gets *better*? What *breaks*?

This is the hardest exercise in governance design, and almost nobody does it honestly. People who hate authority say "everything gets better." People who love authority say "everything breaks." Both are wrong, and they're wrong in the same way: they refuse to engage with the actual tradeoffs.

The truth is some decisions absolutely should move to community vote. Some absolutely shouldn't. The question is *which* — and the only way to figure that out is to test the move on specific examples.

## What the Manifesto actually argues

[**Ch.7**](https://offda9.com/curriculum/observer/the-solution-framework.php) (*The Solution Framework*) and [**Ch.13**](https://offda9.com/curriculum/theorist/scientific-institutions.php) (*Scientific Institution Analysis*) of the Manifesto don't pretend governance is a solved problem. They lay out the actual tradeoffs:

- **Speed vs. legitimacy.** One person can decide in minutes. A community vote takes weeks. For a fire emergency, the community vote loses. For a 50-year zoning policy, the one-person decision is the disaster.
- **Domain expertise vs. lived experience.** The cardiologist knows medicine. The patient knows their life. Most decisions need both — and most existing structures privilege only one.
- **Visibility vs. privacy.** Some decisions need full sunlight to be legitimate. Some decisions involve information that *can't* be public without harming someone. Both pressures are real.

OD9's bet — and it's a bet, not a proof — is that we can design governance mechanisms that hold these tensions instead of collapsing them. The tier-based voting system inside the bot is one experiment. The Pioneer governance work is another. Both are explicitly provisional. We expect to update them as evidence comes in.
