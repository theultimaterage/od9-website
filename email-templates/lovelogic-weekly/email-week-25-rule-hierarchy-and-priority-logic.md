# When two principles fight

Hey {{FIRST_NAME}},

Here's the scenario. Be specific in your head.

A friend or family member you genuinely respect is doing something **legal but clearly self-destructive**. Could be a substance. Could be a relationship. Could be a financial decision that's about to wipe them out. Could be a worldview that's eating them alive. Whatever your version of this is — pick the realest one you've watched in the last few years.

Two OD9 principles are in direct tension here:
- **Protect agency.** They're an adult. Their life. Their call. Pressuring them — or worse, intervening over their objection — would itself be a kind of violence.
- **Prevent harm.** Sitting on your hands while someone you love walks off a cliff is a moral failure, not a moral neutrality.

You can't fully honor both. Whatever you do, you're choosing which principle wins in this case.

Do you intervene? At what point? What's your rule?

This is the hardest design question in any ethical framework. Most belief systems pretend it's solved by appealing to one principle so loudly the other gets drowned out. ("Respect their autonomy!" "Be your brother's keeper!") Both pretend-solutions hide the actual work.

## What the Manifesto pushes on

**Ch.6** of the Manifesto and **Ch.25** take a position that's unusually honest for an ethics-adjacent project: **principles WILL conflict, in concrete cases, predictably.** A system that doesn't tell you which principle wins WHEN — and HOW the priority gets re-evaluated as new evidence arrives — is a system that just dumps the decision on whoever's holding the bag.

The actual design move isn't to remove the conflict. It's to **make the priority logic explicit, defendable, and updateable.** "We protect agency by default. The threshold for overriding is X. Here's who decides. Here's how the decision gets reviewed." That's an institutional design choice, not a vibe.

OD9's tier-based governance is a partial move toward this — higher tiers carry more weight on certain kinds of decisions, contributors who've shown up are weighted more than drive-by opinions, etc. None of it is perfect. All of it is provisional. The point is that there's a *legible* rule, not just whoever-shouts-loudest.

## This week's question

Sunday's deep-dive QOTD:

> *Two OD9 principles in tension: "protect agency" vs. "prevent harm." A member you respect is doing something legal but clearly self-destructive. Do you intervene? At what point? What's your rule?*

The "what's your rule" half is the work. Most people can answer "yes" or "no" for one specific case — the rule that generalizes to *all* such cases is much harder.
