# Where would you actually spend the money?

Hey {{FIRST_NAME}},

Easy mode: complain about education.

Hard mode: redesign it.

Here's the exercise. Imagine a community gives you 100% of its education funding for the next 10 years and lets you spend it however you want. Five buckets to choose from:

- **(a) Better teachers.** Higher pay, better hiring, more retention, real career path.
- **(b) Better materials.** Curricula, textbooks, software, hardware, lab equipment.
- **(c) Smaller classes.** More teachers per kid, more attention, less management overhead.
- **(d) More time.** Longer school year, longer days, year-round schedules.
- **(e) Something not in K-12 at all.** Apprenticeships. Tutoring networks. Community-of-practice models. Library upgrades. Internet access. Free childcare so parents can teach. Anything.

You can split your funding across buckets. But you have to defend the split.

Most people, doing this honestly, end up with answers that look nothing like the current spend. Most public education in the US currently puts huge fractions into administration, infrastructure, and standardized testing infrastructure — three things that almost nobody in the exercise picks. That gap is *the* education conversation, and almost no political discourse engages with it because it threatens too many established interests at once.

## What the Manifesto pushes on

**Ch.8** (*From Vision to Action*) and **Ch.11** of the Manifesto take this seriously. The argument is roughly: **public education in most modern societies is optimizing for industrial-era compliance and credentialing, not for the actual capabilities a Type-I civilization needs**.

Type-I needs: collaborative problem-solvers, long-horizon thinkers, people who can update their beliefs on evidence, people who can coordinate across difference, people who can build and maintain coordination institutions. None of those are what current schooling primarily produces. We get them despite the system, not because of it — and only some of us, unevenly distributed.

That's not a critique of teachers (most of whom are heroes working in a broken system). It's a critique of the *target*. Until we redesign for the actual capabilities we need, we're paying enormous sums to optimize for the wrong outcome.
