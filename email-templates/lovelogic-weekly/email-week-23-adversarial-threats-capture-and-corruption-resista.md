# Not an enemy

Hey {{FIRST_NAME}},

Last week we asked: who tries to co-opt OD9 from the outside? This week, the harder question.

The most dangerous person to OD9 long-term isn't an enemy. **It's a believer who's wrong about something important.**

Describe that person.

Not theoretical. Concrete. Demographic, role, vocabulary, what they get right (so they're trusted), and the specific thing they're wrong about that we'd have a hard time pushing back on without sounding ungrateful or sectarian.

A few archetypes that come up:

- **The Fully-Convinced Founder Loyalist.** Trusts everything The Ultimate Rage says without second-checking. Not malicious — actively helpful in 90% of cases — but their wrong-belief, when it lands, propagates faster than corrections can.
- **The Tech-Solutionist.** Believes most coordination failures are software problems. They're right often enough that it's hard to push back when they're wrong, which is on the cases where the problem is structural, not technical.
- **The Aesthetic Convert.** Loves the vibe — the manifesto, the tier names, the visual identity, the ambition — without engaging much with the actual claims. Brings energy and visibility, but if their wrong-beliefs about the underlying ideas spread, they spread fast and shallow.
- **The Single-Issue Crusader.** Cares deeply about ONE thing OD9 touches — could be UBI, AI safety, a specific governance model, an esoteric philosophical claim. Will quietly steer the conversation toward that issue, year after year, until it dominates.
- **The Domain Expert Who Generalizes Too Far.** Real expertise in one area, applies the frame to areas where it doesn't fit, but the credibility transfer makes it hard to flag.
- **Honestly: any of us, in our blind spot.** The ones we're most sure about are the ones we're least likely to question.

## What the Manifesto pushes on

**Ch.30** and **Ch.52** of the Manifesto take a position that movements rarely articulate publicly: **internal heretics, well-intentioned wrong-believers, and the founder's own blind spots are statistically a bigger threat to the long-run health of a project than external enemies.** External enemies sharpen you. Internal wrong-belief, *if held by people you trust*, gets normalized — and the project drifts toward the wrong-belief without anyone deciding to.

The defense isn't ideological purity tests. (Those guarantee capture by whoever runs the test.) The defense is institutional habits that make wrong-belief easier to surface and update on, even when held by trusted members. **Ch.6**'s "responsible transformation" framework is the manifesto's main move on this. **Ch.52** is more specific about what that looks like during contested periods.
