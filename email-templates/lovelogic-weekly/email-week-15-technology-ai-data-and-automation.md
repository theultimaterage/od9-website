# Pick one.

Hey {{FIRST_NAME}},

Two futures. Both plausible by 2050. You only get one.

**Future A:** AI handles 80% of *decisions* in society. Humans review outcomes after the fact, intervene when something goes wrong, set the broad direction. Most of the moment-to-moment choices — who gets the loan, what treatment to try, what the next policy iteration looks like, who the school district hires — are made by systems. Humans audit.

**Future B:** AI handles 80% of *information* in society. Humans make all the decisions. The systems read everything, summarize, surface patterns, run simulations, draft proposals. But every consequential choice is made by a person looking at the AI's output and saying yes or no. Humans decide.

Which is safer?
Which is humanity worth keeping?

Most people answer those two questions differently and don't notice they did. Future A is "safer" by some measures (consistent, less subject to fatigue, less corruptible by short-term emotion) — but humanity is more clearly worth keeping in Future B (where decisions remain ours, with all the cost that entails). Future B is "less safe" by efficiency measures — but it preserves the thing that makes us responsible for our own civilization.

There's no clean answer. But pretending there is — pretending we're not already drifting toward Future A through default — is how we end up there without ever consciously choosing it.

## What the Manifesto pushes on

**Ch.15** and **Ch.41** of the Manifesto make a position that rubs both AI optimists and AI doomers the wrong way: **technology that increases human agency is good; technology that primarily increases surveillance, dependency, or elite control is bad — even when it's marketed as neutral or beneficial.**

Almost every actual deployed AI system as of 2026 increases dependency on the system's operator more than it increases agency for the end user. Recommendation algorithms decide what you see. Productivity AI decides what your work looks like. Therapy chatbots decide what counts as a healthy thought. None of these are *necessarily* bad — they could be designed otherwise. They're *currently* bad because the optimization target is the platform's interest, not the user's.

A Type-I civilization needs AI that **demonstrably increases human agency and capability** — and rejects the AI that doesn't, even when it's flashy. The discipline is hard to maintain. We're going to have to build a lot of muscle for it.
