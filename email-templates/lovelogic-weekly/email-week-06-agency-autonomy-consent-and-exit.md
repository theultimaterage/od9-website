# Without exit, "consent" is theater

Hey {{FIRST_NAME}},

Name a contract or commitment you're in right now that you genuinely **cannot exit without serious damage**. Not "would be inconvenient." Actual damage — financial, physical, social, legal, or to people you're responsible for.

Most adults can name several within thirty seconds:

- **The lease that costs more to break than to finish.**
- **The job that pays the insurance the kid needs.**
- **The marriage that controls half the assets.**
- **The citizenship of a country whose policies you oppose.**
- **The platform you built your business on.**
- **The "free" service that has 8 years of your data.**

Did you "consent" to those? Technically, yes — at some point in the past, with some level of understanding, you signed up. But the price of leaving is now high enough that the consent has become *retroactive*. You're agreeing to it now because the alternative hurts.

Political philosophers sometimes call this *captive consent* or *manufactured consent*. It's the central problem with most coordination systems we already live in.

## Why this matters for what we're building

[**Ch.7**](https://offda9.com/curriculum/observer/the-solution-framework.php) and [**Ch.16**](https://offda9.com/curriculum/theorist/economic-dysfunction.php) of the Manifesto take this seriously in a way most movements don't. The argument runs roughly: **any system that can't be exited safely is at risk of becoming coercive over time**, regardless of the intent of its founders. Without exit, internal critique loses its teeth. Without internal critique, the system can't update. Without update, it dies — and takes the people inside it down with it.

So if OD9 ever scales — if it becomes a real coordination institution rather than a Discord server — we have to design exit in from the start. Members must be able to leave with their data, their reputation, their contributions intact. The Patreon should be cancelable in two clicks. The community should have no "you can never come back" mechanism. The rules should never assume anyone is locked in.

This sounds obvious. Almost no organization actually does it.
