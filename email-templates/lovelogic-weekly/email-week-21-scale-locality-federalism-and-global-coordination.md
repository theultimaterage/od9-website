# Wrong layer

Hey {{FIRST_NAME}},

Some decisions only make sense in your zip code. Potholes. School district budgets. Whether the corner store should be allowed to sell liquor before 10am. Stuff that depends on context too local to centralize.

Some decisions only make sense at planetary scale. Atmospheric carbon. Antibiotic resistance management. Asteroid deflection budgeting. AGI safety standards. Stuff where any local decision is functionally a free-rider on the rest of the world.

Most decisions in modern life are made at *the wrong layer*. Too local for a problem that's actually planetary. Too global for a problem that depends on context the planetary actor doesn't have. And the mismatch is so consistent that it's the central pattern of why coordination fails in the modern world.

Pick one. Pick a specific decision currently being made at the wrong layer. Where does it belong?

Sample answers I've heard:

- "School curricula are decided at the state level when they should be local-school-and-parent."
- "Carbon taxation is decided at the national level when it has to be planetary or it just leaks."
- "Zoning is decided at the city level when it should be neighborhood-level."
- "AI safety is decided at the company level when it has to be international treaty."
- "Healthcare pricing is decided at the insurance-company level when it should be national or single-payer."

None of those are obviously right. All of them are *coordination problems* dressed up as policy debates — they're really arguments about where the leverage ought to live.

## What the Manifesto pushes on

**Ch.24** and **Ch.47** of the Manifesto take a position that most political movements never quite articulate: **subsidiarity isn't a left-vs-right question — it's a design question, and the right answer depends on the texture of the specific problem.**

Where you place authority for any given decision should be the *lowest* layer at which the decision can be made well — given the information available, the externalities involved, and the affected parties. This is harder than it sounds because you have to be willing to be wrong in BOTH directions: sometimes the answer is "this is too local to scale up," sometimes it's "this is too global to keep local." Movements that always pick "local good, global bad" are wrong about half the time. Same for the reverse.

A Type-I civilization needs operators who can do this analysis decision by decision, without flinching when the answer cuts against their preferred political tribe.
