# What do you actually want?

Hey {{FIRST_NAME}},

Most conversations about "justice" stay abstract enough that nobody has to commit to anything. So let's not.

Imagine someone in your actual circle — friend, family, colleague — does something seriously harmful. Not theoretically. Not "imagine a stranger." Someone you know.

Walk through what you actually want to happen, in order:

- **Punishment?** They suffer in proportion to what they did. Whose hands?
- **Repair?** They make the harmed party whole, as much as possible. How is "whole" defined?
- **Truth-telling?** The full story comes out, publicly. To whom?
- **Exile?** They're removed from the circle. For how long? On what conditions for return?
- **Reintegration?** They're brought back in, eventually, conditional on something. What?

Be honest about the order. Most people, doing this exercise honestly, find their order is *very different* from what the criminal justice system actually optimizes for. Most also find that the order changes depending on the harm — that what they want for petty theft is wildly different from what they want for sexual violence, and what they want for someone they love is different from what they want for a stranger.

Both of those are honest reactions. Designing around them is hard.

## What the Manifesto actually argues

**Ch.18** and [**Ch.22**](https://offda9.com/curriculum/theorist/religious-institutions.php) of the Manifesto take a position most political conversations dodge: **most modern justice systems are optimized for punishment, when what people actually want — when you ask them about people they know — is some combination of repair, truth, and conditional reintegration.**

The mismatch is structural. Punishment is easy to administer at scale. Repair is hard. Truth-telling is hard. Conditional reintegration is hard. So the cheap option (punishment) wins — even though almost no individual would order it first if asked about someone they cared about.

A post-Type-0 justice system has to solve the harder design problem: **how do you operationalize repair-and-reintegration at scale, with enough teeth that perpetrators don't just walk away, and enough mercy that the harmed party isn't betrayed twice?**

OD9 doesn't have a finished answer. Almost nobody does. The best models we have come from restorative justice traditions, transformative justice movements, and a handful of small-scale experiments. All of them break down when scaled too fast. So the work is incremental, careful, and ongoing.
