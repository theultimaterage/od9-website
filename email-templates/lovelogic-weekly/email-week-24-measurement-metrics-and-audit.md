# Goodhart's Law, locally

Hey {{FIRST_NAME}},

You probably know the name. **Goodhart's Law:** "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."

Sounds abstract. Isn't.

Pick one. Name a metric your job, your school, your city, your gym, your gaming group, your therapy app — anything — tracks. A metric that's actively making things WORSE because tracking it changes the behavior of the people being tracked.

Some that come up reliably:

- **Standardized test scores.** Schools optimize for them; what gets taught is what gets tested; the kids who are good at being measured climb; the kids who aren't get sorted into categories that follow them.
- **GitHub commits or "lines of code."** Engineers learn to inflate. Real productivity drops. Managers who reward this get the engineering org they deserve.
- **Customer service "first call resolution."** Reps learn to close tickets without resolving the underlying issue. Customer experience degrades. Bonuses pay out.
- **Police arrest counts.** What gets policed is whatever produces arrests; communities get over-policed in measurable ways and under-policed in immeasurable ones.
- **Hospital readmission rates.** Hospitals learn to discharge patients in ways that reduce 30-day-readmission stats but harm long-term outcomes.
- **Engagement minutes on a social platform.** The platform learns to maximize compulsion. Users' actual lives degrade. Quarterly numbers go up.
- **Steps per day.** People learn to game the step counter. Actual fitness flatlines.

Once you see this pattern, it's everywhere. Almost every modern institution measures something that, when it became a target, broke the substrate it was supposed to be measuring.

## What the Manifesto pushes on

**Ch.24** and **Ch.48** of the Manifesto take a position that's almost embarrassingly underrated in mainstream policy discourse: **the choice of metrics is the most consequential design choice any institution makes.** More than budget. More than hiring. More than mission statement. Because the metric WILL eventually become the target, and the target WILL eventually become what the institution actually does — regardless of stated intent.

A Type-I civilization that doesn't take metric-design seriously is a Type-I civilization that drifts toward whatever happened to be easy to measure. So the design discipline is: **measure things that, even when gamed, still produce the outcome you actually want.** Or measure many things in tension, so optimizing for one trades against another. Or change metrics on a known cadence so nobody can build a 10-year career around gaming a single number.

None of these are easy. All of them are necessary. And almost no institution does the work.
