# What looks obviously wrong in 100 years

Hey {{FIRST_NAME}},

This is the most charged email in the campaign. We've put it off until week 30 on purpose. By now you know the voice; you know we don't dodge.

The frame:

In 1955, doctors smoked in delivery rooms. Most of them. Cigarette companies advertised in medical journals. Pediatricians appeared in cigarette ads recommending specific brands as "less irritating." This was *normal* and *unremarkable* — the ambient assumption was that smoking was fine, possibly healthy, certainly the doctor's business and not the patient's.

We look back at this and feel a kind of vertigo. Not "they were stupid" — they weren't. They were operating with a model of reality their culture had handed them, and the model was wrong in a way that's now obvious in hindsight.

**100 years from now, what is something about how the current mainstream handles sex, gender, desire, or reproduction that our descendants will look back on with that same kind of vertigo?**

This question is unusually hard because:
- The current discourse on these topics is *politically loaded in every direction*. Whatever you say, some segment of OD9's audience is going to find it a stretch.
- The "obviously wrong in hindsight" frame is honest about uncertainty. We don't actually KNOW what'll look bad in 100 years. We're being asked to make a prediction, knowing we might be wrong.
- The honest answers cut against current consensus on EITHER side of any culture-war framing. Most members will find that uncomfortable.

Be willing to commit to your answer anyway.

Some honest attempts I've heard, across the political spectrum, no editorializing:

- *"That we let teenagers make permanent medical decisions about themselves when their adult brains aren't yet present."*
- *"That we restricted teenagers from making decisions about their own bodies that adults handle as routine."*
- *"That sexual partners had no enforceable obligation to pre-disclose STI status."*
- *"That we treated child-rearing as a private burden instead of as load-bearing public infrastructure."*
- *"That porn was monetized at industrial scale with zero accountability to the people whose images and labor it consumed."*
- *"That dating apps were optimized for the platforms' engagement, not for anyone's actual mate-finding."*
- *"That divorce defaulted to adversarial litigation instead of structured repair."*
- *"That birth control's side effects were never studied with anything close to the rigor we'd now demand."*

Some of those will land for you. Some will offend you. That's the point of the exercise — being honest about what YOU think will look bad, even when your answer doesn't fit a tribe.

## What the Manifesto pushes on

**Ch.12** and **Ch.43** of the Manifesto take a position most movements wisely avoid: **questions about sex, reproduction, and family will be central to any successful Type-I civilization, AND the current discourse on them is not equipped to answer those questions well.** The manifesto doesn't propose answers — it tries to set up the design space honestly so future iterations can.

This is one of the few areas where OD9's "we might be wrong" stance isn't a humility move, it's a survival move. The most likely failure mode for a movement engaging these questions is to lock in a position that's either (a) downstream of present-moment culture-war framings, or (b) a deliberate contrarian stance that ages badly. Both are reliable ways to end up looking like the doctors in the delivery rooms.
